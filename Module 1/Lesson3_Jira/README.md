# Head First Agile — Chapters 1–3 Summary

*Head First Agile* by Andrew Stellman and Jennifer Greene (2017). Summary made from the uploaded PDF, which stops partway through Chapter 3 at the book's page 85.

These chapters argue that Agile works only when the team shares the mindset behind the practices, then lay out the Agile Manifesto and the rules of Scrum.

---

## Chapter 1: What is Agile? (Principles and practices)

The main idea is that **Agile is a mindset, not just a set of practices.** A practice only works well when the whole team believes in it.

- **The story:** Kate is a project manager at a startup. Her boss Ben, the product owner, wants new features fast. Mike, the lead programmer, says no: the change came too late, and they'd have to rip out a lot of code.
- **The first try fails:** Kate starts a **daily standup**, a short meeting where everyone stands so it stays quick. But she uses it to check whether people are following *her* plan. The developers see it as one more pointless meeting, look at their phones, and say as little as possible.
- **What fixes it:** a change in attitude. Kate starts treating the plan as something the *whole team* builds together. Mike sees the standup as a way to steer the project. The meeting starts catching problems early. In the story, a developer flags an architecture problem, so they cut two features instead of working late nights.
- **What Agile is:** a set of simple methods aimed at real software problems, plus a shared mindset. Information flows openly, and the whole team helps decide things instead of just the boss.
- **The three main approaches:**
    - **Scrum**, the most common, is about project management.
    - **XP** is about writing good code.
    - **Lean/Kanban** is about steadily improving how the team works.
- **Practices introduced:** user stories, task boards, burndown charts, refactoring and retrospectives.
- **Warning:** a Scrum sprint is *not* the same as an old-style project phase. A traditional plan maps out every phase at the start. Scrum only plans the next sprint in detail.

## Chapter 2: Agile values and principles (Mindset meets method)

**The Agile Manifesto (2001):** seventeen leaders of the newer "lightweight" methods met at the Snowbird ski resort in Utah. They agreed on four values:

| Agile teams value… | …more than |
| --- | --- |
| Individuals and interactions | Processes and tools |
| Working software | Thorough documentation |
| Customer collaboration | Contract negotiation |
| Responding to change | Following a plan |

The key point is that the things on the right still matter. Agile teams simply value the left side *more*. The book says Scrum teams actually do *more* planning than waterfall teams. It just doesn't feel like it, because they adapt as they go.

- **Waterfall:** the traditional method, where you write all the requirements, then do all the design, then write the code. It's not "bad," but problems often show up only once coding starts, when they're expensive to fix.
- **A cautionary story:** Mike's team spends a year building a feature that tracks down listeners' identities. By release time, clients have been sued over privacy, so nobody wants it. The lesson is to deliver early and talk to users often, or you risk building the wrong thing.
- **The 12 principles,** grouped the way the book explains them:
    - **Delivery:** keep users happy by delivering valuable software early and often, every few weeks if possible. Welcome changes, even late ones. Working software is the real measure of progress. Delivering often means mistakes are caught before a lot of code has to be redone, and less rework means fewer bugs.
    - **Teamwork:** business people and developers work together every day. Give motivated people support and trust. Talking face to face beats long specification documents.
    - **Sustainability and quality:** keep a pace you can maintain for the long run, with no endless overtime. Pay attention to good design. Keep things simple and skip work that isn't needed. Let teams organize themselves, and stop regularly to look back and improve.
- **Practices that put the principles to work:**
    - **Iteration:** short cycles with a hard deadline (a "timebox"). If something doesn't fit, it moves to the next cycle.
    - **Backlog:** the list of features waiting to be built. Anything not yet in a cycle can still be changed.
- **Motivation:** fear, blame and 70-hour weeks drain teams. A change shouldn't be treated as a mistake; usually people just learned something new.

## Chapter 3: Managing projects with Scrum (The rules of Scrum)

The example team here is Ranch Hand Games, makers of the fictional *Cows Gone Wild* games. They nearly missed their last deadline working 90-hour weeks, so they try Scrum. Amy becomes Product Owner and Rick becomes Scrum Master.

**The three roles:**

- **Product Owner:** decides what gets built and keeps the backlog ordered by value. This must be one person, not a committee.
- **Scrum Master:** a "servant leader" who helps everyone, including managers, use Scrum properly.
- **Development Team:** everyone else. A team has at least 3 people and usually no more than 9.

**The three artifacts:**

- **Product Backlog:** everything the product might need, each with an order, description, effort estimate and value. It's never finished.
- **Sprint Backlog:** the items picked for this sprint, the Sprint Goal, and the tasks they're broken into.
- **Increment:** all the working software finished so far.

**The five events, all timeboxed:**

| Event | What happens | Timebox |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sprint | One cycle, often 2 weeks or 30 days | Fixed length |
| Sprint Planning | First half: set the Sprint Goal and choose items. Second half: break items into tasks of a day or less | 8 hours for a 30-day sprint, 4 hours for a 2-week sprint |
| Daily Scrum | Three questions: What did I do? What will I do next? What's blocking me? | 15 minutes |
| Sprint Review | Show the working software to stakeholders and get feedback | 4 hours for a 30-day sprint |
| Sprint Retrospective | What went well, and what will we improve? | 3 hours for a 30-day sprint |

**Following the rules isn't enough.** Four months in, the team is struggling:

- Amy picked a feature for the sprint, and the CEO yelled at her because she supposedly had no authority to decide that.
- Programmers are skipping the Daily Scrum.

The fix is the **five Scrum values**:

- **Openness:** be honest about problems.
- **Respect:** trust your teammates.
- **Courage:** say no to impossible goals.
- **Focus:** do one task at a time; multitasking delays everything.
- **Commitment:** the team owns the whole result, and the company backs it by giving the Product Owner real authority.

The chapter ends (in this PDF) with the pig-and-chicken fable. In a bacon-and-eggs breakfast, the chicken is *involved* but the pig is *committed*.
